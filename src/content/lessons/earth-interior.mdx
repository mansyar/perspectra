Earth is not a uniform ball of rock. A thin crust surrounds a thick silicate mantle, which in turn
surrounds a metallic core. The six landmarks below separate those compositional layers from the
lithosphere, a mechanical layer that crosses the crust–mantle boundary. Each landmark provides an
overview, a closer detail, and context for how it fits the whole planet.

<div id="crust" data-hotspot="crust">
  <h2>Crust</h2>
  <p data-layer="overview">
    <strong>Overview.</strong> The crust is Earth's thin, rocky outer shell. It is far thinner than
    the mantle or core, but it is the layer where people live and where most of the planet's
    accessible rock record is found.
  </p>
  <p data-layer="detail">
    <strong>Detail.</strong> Oceanic crust is generally thinner and denser than continental crust.
    Their different compositions and buoyancy help shape the elevations of ocean basins and
    continents.
  </p>
  <p data-layer="context">
    <strong>Context.</strong> The crust is only one part of the lithosphere. That rigid outer layer
    also includes the uppermost mantle, so “crust” and “lithosphere” do not describe the same
    boundary.
  </p>
</div>

<div id="upper_mantle" data-hotspot="upper_mantle">
  <h2>Upper mantle</h2>
  <p data-layer="overview">
    <strong>Overview.</strong> The upper mantle lies beneath the crust and extends to roughly 660
    kilometers deep. It is mostly solid silicate rock, not a global ocean of magma.
  </p>
  <p data-layer="detail">
    <strong>Detail.</strong> Over geological time, hot mantle rock can flow slowly under sustained
    stress. This plastic behavior allows convection and helps transfer heat from Earth's interior
    toward the surface.
  </p>
  <p data-layer="context">
    <strong>Context.</strong> Its uppermost portion belongs to the rigid lithosphere. Below that
    lies the weaker asthenosphere, which allows tectonic plates to move while the mantle remains
    mostly solid.
  </p>
</div>

<div id="lower_mantle" data-hotspot="lower_mantle">
  <h2>Lower mantle</h2>
  <p data-layer="overview">
    <strong>Overview.</strong> The lower mantle is the thickest of Earth's major interior layers. It
    lies between the upper mantle and the core-mantle boundary and consists mainly of solid silicate
    rock under immense pressure.
  </p>
  <p data-layer="detail">
    <strong>Detail.</strong> Minerals in the lower mantle adopt denser structures as pressure
    increases with depth. Slow circulation still carries heat, but the rock does not behave like a
    liquid magma layer.
  </p>
  <p data-layer="context">
    <strong>Context.</strong> The lower mantle connects the relatively accessible upper mantle to
    the metallic core. Changes in its mineral structure also alter how seismic waves travel through
    the deep Earth.
  </p>
</div>

<div id="outer_core" data-hotspot="outer_core">
  <h2>Outer core</h2>
  <p data-layer="overview">
    <strong>Overview.</strong> The outer core is a layer of liquid iron and nickel surrounding the
    inner core. It begins beneath the mantle at the core-mantle boundary.
  </p>
  <p data-layer="detail">
    <strong>Detail.</strong> The liquid metal is extremely hot but remains fluid because pressure
    there is lower than inside the inner core. Its motion is driven by heat loss and compositional
    changes as Earth cools.
  </p>
  <p data-layer="context">
    <strong>Context.</strong> Circulating electrically conductive metal in the outer core sustains
    Earth's magnetic field. Seismic shear waves cannot pass through this liquid layer, one key line
    of evidence for its state.
  </p>
</div>

<div id="inner_core" data-hotspot="inner_core">
  <h2>Inner core</h2>
  <p data-layer="overview">
    <strong>Overview.</strong> The inner core is a solid sphere of iron and nickel at Earth's
    center. It is solid despite its extraordinary temperature because the pressure is even more
    extreme.
  </p>
  <p data-layer="detail">
    <strong>Detail.</strong> As Earth cools, more liquid iron solidifies at the inner core's
    boundary. The growing crystal structure and its interaction with the outer core can influence
    the planet's magnetic field.
  </p>
  <p data-layer="context">
    <strong>Context.</strong> Seismic waves reveal a distinct inner-core boundary and show that the
    center transmits shear waves. Together, those observations support a solid inner core enclosed
    by a liquid outer core.
  </p>
</div>

<div id="lithosphere" data-hotspot="lithosphere">
  <h2>Lithosphere</h2>
  <p data-layer="overview">
    <strong>Overview.</strong> The lithosphere is Earth's rigid outer layer, made of the crust and
    the uppermost mantle. It is divided into tectonic plates that move relative to one another.
  </p>
  <p data-layer="detail">
    <strong>Detail.</strong> Oceanic and continental lithosphere both include mantle beneath their
    crust, but they differ in thickness, age, density, and composition. Those differences affect how
    plates interact at their boundaries.
  </p>
  <p data-layer="context">
    <strong>Context.</strong> “Lithosphere” describes strength and mechanical behavior, not chemical
    composition. It overlaps the crust and upper mantle rather than forming a separate compositional
    shell between them.
  </p>
</div>
